Network Strategy Lab
Baum, Cowan & Jonard (2014) · “Does Evidence of Network Effects on Firm Performance in Pooled Cross-Section Support Prescriptions for Network Strategy?” · Strategic Management Journal 35: 652–667

Network Strategy Lab

Studies of alliance networks often find that firms in open, brokering positions outperform firms in closed, dense ones, and conclude that firms should seek open positions. This paper asks whether that inference holds. If open positions raise the variance of performance rather than its mean, they will look good in any snapshot of top performers and still fail to pay over a firm’s history. The three parts below follow the paper’s argument: a stripped-down version of the variance logic, the alliance network model itself, and the comparison of cross-section with long-run strategy.

Part 1Why a snapshot can mislead

Before building the network model, the paper makes its point with a much simpler one. A hundred firms each carry a fixed characteristic c between 1 and C. Each period one firm is drawn and paid either a high amount (1.01c) or a low one (0.01c). The odds of the high payoff fall as c rises, exactly enough that every firm expects to earn 1 per draw. Only the variance differs.

Each dot is one simulated population. A cross-section correlation pairs each individual payoff with the characteristic of the firm that received it. A long-run correlation pairs each firm’s total over 2,000 periods with its characteristic.

What to look for. As the population distribution becomes more right-skewed, the highest payoffs at any moment belong more and more to high-variance firms, so the cross-section correlation climbs. Over the long run, every firm earns the same, and the correlation sits near zero.

Part 2Watch an alliance network evolve

A hundred firms sit in a two-dimensional knowledge space that wraps around at the edges (a torus). Two firms ally when both expect the alliance to pay, which happens when their knowledge is neither too similar nor too different; the optimal distance is d* = 0.025. Allied firms learn from each other and drift together.

Alliances occasionally produce an innovation. It forces every other firm to redeploy its knowledge, moving it a random distance in a random direction, and firms closer to the innovator move further. The innovator earns the total displacement it causes.

C sets the innovation regime. An innovation has size H = 1.01C with small probability, otherwise L = 0.01C; the expected size is always 1. Low C: steady, comparable innovations, an exploitation regime. High C: mostly trivial innovations and the occasional huge one, an exploration regime.

What to look for. Compare C = 5 with C = 20 (the paper’s Figures 3 and 4). At low C, degree and clustering bounce around constantly. At high C, learning dominates for long stretches, so clusters tighten and degree and clustering climb, until a rare large innovation (marked in red) scatters firms and knocks both down.

Part 3Cross-section versus strategy

Each dot is one industry run with C drawn at random. Two correlations come out of every run.

The cross-section correlation is innovation-centric, like a pooled study: across all innovations, the innovator’s profit against its network position at that moment.

The strategy correlation is firm-centric: across firms, each firm’s total profit over the run against its average position over the run. This is the question a firm choosing a network strategy needs answered.

What to look for. The paper finds that open positions (high betweenness) go with higher profit in cross-section, more so as skew rises, while the strategy correlation falls with skew and turns negative. Constraint, the measure of closure, reverses both. The table compares each slope with the paper’s Table 1.

Add your own runs

How this version follows the paper, and where it departs. Parameters are the paper’s: 100 firms, d* = σ = 0.025, maximum success rate 0.004, maximum dislocation 0.05, absorptive capacity 0.01, alliance cost 0.000001, and the first 500 periods discarded. Where the paper leaves a detail open, this version makes a choice. A firm with several partners moves toward their average position (the paper states learning for a single pair). Network position is sampled every 10 periods for firm averages, and constraint uses equal tie weights with isolated firms left out. C is drawn between 2 and 30, because near C = 1 the profit distribution turns left-skewed. Skew is measured on innovation-level profits, which reproduces the paper’s reported skews reasonably closely. The networks here run a little sparser and more innovative than the paper reports (average degree about 3.5 against about 5, and 0.4–0.5 innovations per period against about 0.3). Runs are also shorter and fewer than the paper’s 500 runs of 10,000 periods, so the firm-level strategy correlations in particular are noisy. In the opening sample of 150 runs, three of the four predictions in the paper’s Table 1 come through clearly (both cross-section patterns, and the falling strategy correlation for betweenness). The strategy correlation for constraint shows no clear trend, and the variance check in the second chart stays near zero instead of starting positive at low skew as in the paper’s Figure 7. Those two gaps are worth discussing with students as a question about how sensitive a model’s firm-level results are to unstated implementation details.
